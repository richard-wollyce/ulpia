The memory is yours. The model is a guest.

Your coding agent answered out of the wrong file and sounded certain. Ulpia is the memory it asks instead: your own markdown, indexed where it already sits, answering with the files to open, the words that matched, and a confidence you can check.

A library that lives with you.

Ulpia is a library. Your notes, your decisions, your protocols are its books: markdown files, in folders, on your disk. Move the folder and everything moves with it, because no absolute path exists anywhere inside a fleet.

Version it with git if you want the history; nothing is served on that condition. A folder with a note in it is already a base, with no git init, no manifest and no marker.

Vesta is its librarian. Ask her a question and she answers with the files that should open, and no model reads your library to do it. When she is wrong, she says why, in words you can act on.

Named for the Bibliotheca Ulpia, Trajan's library: Rome's reading room and its record office in one building. That pair, the knowledge and the record of the decisions behind it, is what this software holds.

What you would use it instead of.

Two kinds of tool get used as a project's memory, and they lose for two different reasons. One is worth swapping and the other is worth keeping, so it is worth being exact about which is which.

A notes app, and Obsidian is the one people name. It keeps markdown on your disk and it is good at what it is built for: graph view, backlinks, canvas, sync, a plugin ecosystem years deep. Ulpia has none of those. If your job is reading your own notes with your own eyes, keep it.

The mechanism is who the organising is aimed at. Every one of those features is drawn for a person to look at, and none of them is a call an agent can make. The agent working in your repository opens a shell, not a vault, so what it does with a folder of markdown is grep it, and grep has no opinion about which file is the right one. Ulpia organises the same files for the query instead of for the eye.

A vector memory layer. It fixes the matching, which is real, and it leaves the certainty alone. Retrieval comes back as a ranking, and a ranking has a top entry whether or not the corpus holds the answer, so the confident wrong citation survives the upgrade. It also ingests without asking you to write anything, which Ulpia does not, and that is a genuine advantage rather than a concession.

What is ours is the middle of that comparison rather than the end of it. Nothing in the retrieval path guesses, which is what lets one answer be explainable, reproducible and offline at the same time, and those three are what you feel on an ordinary Tuesday. The last one follows from them: a score you can trust is also a score that can come up short, and on LongMemEval, a public benchmark of 500 instances, the fleet got that call right 29 times out of 30 on 24 August 2026. The same run answered 51 of 56 on single-session-assistant, and the two figures travel together so neither reads as one picked out of a table.

Watch her work.

Two independent scorers read every question. One is a keyword scorer over the Search for line each note carries under its title, the label on the book. The other is full text search over the content. The verdict is hit, guess or nothing, and it comes from one number: the top file's keyword score, measured against a floor that scales with the size of the library. Whether the second scorer found the same file is reported beside it, as in the last column above, and never as the verdict. The number printed above is the fused score of both rankings; the keyword score behind the verdict, and the floor it was measured against, are printed by kb route --json.

A miss is answered too. Rather than returning an empty screen, the library offers the keys it does hold that look like the words you used, so you learn what it covers instead of learning to stop asking. That comparison is spelling and not meaning: it reaches a typo or a cognate and never a translation, and the reply says which kind of help it is.

And when a subject belongs to nobody in your fleet, Vesta says exactly that: nothing in this fleet covers it, which is a gap in the fleet rather than a gap in your question. No score can produce that sentence. It is a judgement about a subject, and the section on the guest says who makes it.

How long one question takes.

A hosted memory layer embeds your question, crosses a network, searches, ranks and comes back. This one reads an index that lives beside the files it indexes. The gap is not an optimisation, it is the two mechanisms, and it is the reason the bar below is a hairline.

The other trade.

Nothing is free. Putting an embedding model between you and your own notes buys semantic matching, and the bill is four things: you cannot run it offline, you cannot explain a bad result, you cannot reproduce yesterday's answer, and your notes have been somewhere you did not choose.

Writing a line of keys on each note is a smaller bill, paid earlier, and this is what it buys back.

  • Offline, because your files are read on your machine and nowhere else.
  • Explainable, because the answer names the words that matched under each file, and says when a file was found by its text alone.
  • Reproducible, because the same question finds the same files, today and in a year.
  • Private, because each base declares its own private layer, and Vesta serves nothing inside it unless the owner asks for it with --all.

Those four are properties of how your notes are read. The one step that may call a model is about your fleet and not your notes, and it is next.

The model is a guest.

Every memory product offers the same bargain: useful context, in exchange for your notes living somewhere you do not control. Ulpia gives you the context and leaves the ownership where it already was. What follows is the mechanism that makes both true at once.

Which file holds the answer and which of your agents should give it are different questions. Finding the file is a lookup, and code does that exactly. Choosing the agent is a judgement about a subject, so a model does that one.

That model is handed your question, the roster, the file names Vesta found with the words that matched, and the one line at the top of each of those files where you said what it is for. Never the files themselves. It reads a briefing at the door, never the shelves.

The step calls a command you name, which you can swap or remove; with none configured, the keyword scores pick the agent on their own. It takes seconds, and moving it onto your own machine is the work in front of us. The code is open source under Apache 2.0.

A model may stand between you and your fleet. Never between you and your notes. Then you hand the files to whatever model you like: yours, locally; Claude, through MCP; something else next year. The memory outlives the model.

Where it stands.

Early, used daily, and honest about which is which. The core is tested, and no count is printed because the number moves between sessions. The tray app is Windows only and young. There is no macOS binary and no hosted instance, and local model routing is not built. The source is public at github.com/richard-wollyce/ulpia.

If that paragraph made you more curious instead of less, this is being built for you.

The first four pages of the docs are written to be read in order, and say beside each how long it takes. The benchmarks put every number of ours beside its command, machine and date, and the commit where the results file records one.

No cookies. One counter, and it is cookieless.

This page sets no cookies. Everything it loads comes from ulpia.io except one script: Cloudflare Web Analytics, which counts page views. It uses no cookie and no local storage, and it builds no profile and no identifier that would follow you to the next site. That is the whole of it, and it is the only thing here from anyone else. The stylesheet, the small scripts and the three EB Garamond files are ours, served from ulpia.io under /assets/.

It makes exactly one request that loading the page did not make, and only if you ask for it: when you submit the form, your address is posted to ulpia.io and stored in Cloudflare D1, with the time and the version of the consent sentence you ticked, so that we can write to you. No IP address is recorded. Nothing else goes with it, and nothing goes at all until you submit.

The button beside our address copies it to your clipboard when you press it. It writes; it never reads.

If you choose a light or dark scheme, that choice is kept in your browser's local storage under the key ulpia-scheme and never leaves your machine.

The page is served by Cloudflare Pages, which means Cloudflare terminates TLS and sees the request. That is the one third party in the path and we would rather name it here than have you find it.

What happens to an address afterwards is in the privacy notice. The product holds the same position as the page.
